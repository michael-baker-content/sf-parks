## A photograph is more than decoration

Park data can tell me that a destination has a lawn, a path, or a recreation facility. A photograph can do something different: it can help a visitor understand the slope of the land, the amount of tree cover, or the relationship between a park and its neighborhood.

That makes photography valuable to this project, especially while I build a collection of original images. In the meantime, I use second-hand photographs selectively. I am not looking for any attractive image that happens to appear in a search result. I am looking for photographs whose reuse terms are clear and whose subject genuinely helps someone understand the place.

## Creative Commons is permission with conditions

When a photographer releases an image under a Creative Commons license, I treat that choice as an invitation with responsibilities attached. Before adding a photograph, I review its source page, record the stated license, preserve the creator's credit, and link back to both the original record and the license.

Different licenses carry different requirements. Some permit reuse with attribution. Share-alike licenses also ask adaptations to remain available under compatible terms. Public-domain and CC0 records allow broader reuse, but I still preserve source information whenever it is available. Clear provenance is useful even when attribution is not legally required.

This is also why the site does not copy an image without keeping its context. Each approved photograph has a caption, alternative text, creator or contributor credit, source link, license link, and review record.

## Relevance matters as much as image quality

A technically impressive photograph is not automatically the right photograph. I prefer images that show the park itself rather than a distant skyline, a nearby landmark, or a dramatic panorama that makes the location difficult to recognize. When several images are available, I try to lead with one that fits the site's layout and gives the clearest first impression.

The photographs highlighted above reflect several kinds of reusable material already represented in the project: a CC0 view of an Alamo Square entrance, an attributed photograph of Stern Grove, a share-alike image of Huntington Falls, and a community-contributed LocalWiki view of Portsmouth Square. Their licenses differ, but the same basic practice applies to all of them: verify, credit, link, and use the image for a real informational purpose.

## Building toward an original collection

Reusable photography gives this project a responsible starting point, not necessarily its final visual identity. I would still like to add more original photographs, particularly for destinations that are poorly represented online. Those images can focus on the practical questions this site is trying to answer: What does the entrance look like? Is the terrain steep? Where are the main gathering areas? What might help someone recognize that they have arrived?

Until that collection grows, careful reuse lets the site become more useful without treating creative work as anonymous raw material. The goal is not simply to fill empty rectangles. It is to make each page more informative while respecting the people who made that possible.
